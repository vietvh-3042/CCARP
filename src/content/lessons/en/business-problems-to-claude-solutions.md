leading-relaxed text-foreground">A business problem arrives as a complaint, not as a spec. Tickets land with the wrong team, support queues overflow during a launch, or lawyers spend hours summarising contracts. Asking "which model?" at this point skips the real question: which part of the current process causes the pain? Anthropic's ticket-routing guide opens by investigating the existing system, before any prompt is written. It asks how the support team routes tickets today, what decides the SLA a ticket gets, and whether the routing chooses a support tier or a product specialist.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">The most revealing question is where the current automation breaks. If rules or workflows are already in place, find out exactly where they fail and how the team handles edge cases and ambiguous tickets. Those failure points are usually where the value sits. A keyword rule that sends every ticket mentioning "payment" to billing works until a customer writes about a payment-page bug. That ticket is a software problem, and a human fixes the routing by hand. Each manual correction like that is a cost you can count, and a step Claude might take over.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Once the process is mapped, break the target interaction into separate tasks. Customer support chat looks like one feature, but it is a collection of multiple different tasks, from question answering to information retrieval to taking action on requests. Anthropic's worked example is a car-insurance support chatbot: a customer opens a chat, asks about coverage for a new electric car, drifts into an off-topic question, then asks for a quote. Its tasks are greeting, product information, conversation management (staying on topic about car insurance) and quote generation through an API. Each task gets its own prompt and its own evaluation. You cannot test "the chatbot" as a whole, but you can test whether quote generation asks the right eligibility questions.</p><aside class="my-6 rounded-lg border border-dashed border-border bg-muted/20 p-4"><p class="flex items-center gap-1.5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-muted-foreground"><svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" class="lucide lucide-circle-help h-3.5 w-3.5 shrink-0" aria-hidden="true"><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="10"></circle><path d="M9.09 9a3 3 0 0 1 5.83 1c0 2-3 3-3 3"></path><path d="M12 17h.01"></path></svg>Apply it to your own work</p><p class="mt-1.5 text-sm leading-relaxed text-foreground">Pick a process you know well, such as invoice approval, onboarding or incident triage. tabular-nums font-normal text-muted-foreground">2<!-- -->.</span>Decide which steps Claude should do</h2><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Not every step in the map belongs to an LLM. Claude earns a step when the work involves language, judgement or variety that rigid systems handle badly. For ticket classification, Anthropic lists concrete signals that favour Claude over traditional ML:</p><ul class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground list-disc space-y-1 pl-6"><li class="pl-1">Labelled data is scarce: Claude can classify with a few dozen labelled examples, where traditional ML needs massive labelled datasets.</li><li class="pl-1">Categories change often: Claude adapts to new or redefined classes without extensive relabelling.</li><li class="pl-1">Inputs are unstructured, or classes are defined by conditions rather than by examples.</li><li class="pl-1">Decisions must be explainable: Claude can give human-readable reasons for each classification.</li><li class="pl-1">Tickets arrive in many languages, and you do not want a separate model for each one.</li></ul><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">In support chat, the signals relate to volume and coverage. Claude handles a large number of similar questions efficiently, which frees human agents for complex issues. It also provides round-the-clock coverage and absorbs sudden spikes without new hires. If none of these apply, Claude may not be the right tool for the step. A stable, well-labelled, single-language classification problem that a traditional model already solves is one example.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">The model's limits shape the design too. A language model produces text by predicting the next token, so every answer is the most plausible continuation, not a looked-up fact. That works well for summaries and classifications, and it fails in predictable places: a citation that reads correctly but does not exist, a precise figure or name the model never learned, anything that happened after its knowledge cutoff, and details specific to your company that were never in its training data. Where coverage is thin, the model still writes fluent text, so fabrication looks the same as accuracy. A step that needs current or company-specific facts is therefore designed with retrieval, web search or a tool from the start. Any step whose output cannot be checked needs a human or a programmatic gate after it.</p><aside class="my-6 rounded-lg border-l-4 border-l-primary border-y border-r border-border bg-primary/5 p-4"><p class="mb-1.5 flex items-center gap-1.5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-primary"><svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" class="lucide lucide-target h-3.5 w-3.5 shrink-0" aria-hidden="true"><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="10"></circle><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="6"></circle><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="2"></circle></svg>On the exam</p><p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-foreground">When a scenario describes the business problem, look for the answer that changes the painful step and leaves the working steps alone. text-foreground">A solution is judged by whether it relieves the pain found in the process map, not by how well the model behaves in isolation. Good criteria are specific, measurable, achievable and relevant, as taught in <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-2 hover:no-underline" href="/en/lessons/writing-measurable-requirements-and-acceptance-tests-for-ai-solutions/">measurable success criteria</a>. The relevance part matters most here: each criterion has to trace back to a business outcome that someone in the organisation already tracks.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">The support guides keep two lists apart. Task criteria cover the model's work, such as intent-comprehension accuracy of 95% or higher, or 100% accuracy on generated quotes. Business-impact criteria cover the outcome the business pays for. For a support chatbot, that means a deflection rate of typically 70–80% of inquiries handled without a human, a CSAT of 4 out of 5 or higher, and a lower average handling time than human agents. For ticket routing, the business measures include a 10–20% improvement in tickets resolved per agent, an escalation rate below 20%, and a 10–15% reduction in cost per ticket.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Each task metric should explain how it moves a business metric. If it cannot, it is a vanity metric. Routing accuracy matters because misrouted tickets get escalated and handled twice. So report routing accuracy next to the escalation rate and cost per ticket it is meant to reduce. Define both lists with the support team that owns the process, not only with the engineers building the solution.
