model trade-offs into a plain recommendation</h2><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Every architectural decision in a Claude solution trades a few things against each other: capability, speed, cost and risk. The person who approves the budget does not need the whole trade-off space. They need one recommendation, what it costs, and the condition that would change it. Engineers need the same decision explained through its mechanism. Communicating a trade-off means saying both versions accurately, and the model choice is usually the first place this comes up.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Each Claude model balances capabilities, speed and cost differently, as covered in <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-2 hover:no-underline" href="/en/lessons/choosing-a-claude-model-lineup-trade-offs-and-starting-points/">Claude model lineup and its intelligence/speed/cost trade-offs</a>. For an executive audience, turn those three axes into one sentence that has a reason and an exit condition. For example: "We start the ticket-triage step on Claude Haiku 5.5 because it is high-volume, straightforward work where latency and price matter most. We move up a tier only if our tests show a specific capability gap." That sentence follows the cost-first path: begin with Haiku 5.5, test thoroughly, and upgrade only where a gap appears. The capability-first path suits complex reasoning or high-autonomy agentic work. It reads differently: start on Claude Opus 5.5 because accuracy outweighs cost here, then lower effort or downgrade once the workflow is tuned.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Present effort as the first dial, before any model switch. Several Claude models expose an effort parameter that trades intelligence for latency and cost within the same model. When a stakeholder says the system costs too much, the honest first answer is "we lower effort and re-measure", not "we move to a cheaper model". Speed can also be bought outright. Fast mode on Opus 5.5, Opus 5 and Opus 4.8 delivers up to 2.5x higher output speed at premium pricing. Put it to stakeholders as a direct exchange of money for response time.</p><aside class="my-6 rounded-lg border border-dashed border-border bg-muted/20 p-4"><p class="flex items-center gap-1.5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-muted-foreground"><svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" class="lucide lucide-circle-help h-3.5 w-3.5 shrink-0" aria-hidden="true"><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="10"></circle><path d="M9.09 9a3 3 0 0 1 5.83 1c0 2-3 3-3 3"></path><path d="M12 17h.01"></path></svg>Apply it to your own work</p><p class="mt-1.5 text-sm leading-relaxed text-foreground">Take a model decision you have recently made or seen made. text-foreground">Architects are rarely asked to justify a simpler design, but they should be ready to justify every layer they add. Matching complexity to the requirement is the principle in <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-2 hover:no-underline" href="/en/lessons/augmented-llm-workflows-or-agents-choosing-the-base-pattern/">Start simple: match complexity to the requirement</a>. Agentic systems often trade latency and cost for better task performance. That sentence is the one to put in front of a steering committee: the extra capability is real, and so is the bill.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Describe the options as a ladder.
