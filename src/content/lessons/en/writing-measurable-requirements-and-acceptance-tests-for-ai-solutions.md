-->.</span>The discovery brief: users, needs, experience, constraints</h2><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Structured discovery is the work you do before designing anything. You collect a fixed set of facts about who the system serves and what limits it, so every later decision can be checked against them. A request such as "patients need to check wait times before coming in" is where the conversation starts, not a requirement. To turn it into a brief, answer four questions in plain language:</p><ul class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground list-disc space-y-1 pl-6"><li class="pl-1"><strong class="font-semibold">Users.</strong> Be specific. Which patients? What about the staff? Who else touches the system?</li><li class="pl-1"><strong class="font-semibold">Needs.</strong> What outcome are they hoping for? This is not the same as the feature they asked for.</li><li class="pl-1"><strong class="font-semibold">Experience.</strong> What does a great solution feel like to use? Describe the experience, not the features.</li><li class="pl-1"><strong class="font-semibold">Constraints.</strong> Budget, the technical skill of the people who will run it, users' access to devices, and privacy requirements.</li></ul><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">A process map is the discovery technique that keeps the brief honest. The idea that <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-2 hover:no-underline" href="/en/lessons/from-business-problem-to-claude-solution-design/">you start from the business process, not the model</a> becomes a concrete step in discovery. Break the user's need into steps, name the stakeholder at each step, and only then decide what role AI plays in each one. There are three roles to choose from: <strong class="font-semibold">Automation</strong> (AI does it and you check), <strong class="font-semibold">Augmentation</strong> (you and AI work on it together) and <strong class="font-semibold">Agency</strong> (AI acts with latitude inside boundaries you set). This produces a small table, one row per step, and the AI decision is made separately for each row. Asking "should we use AI here?" about the whole system skips that step-by-step choice.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Three more questions belong in every first session, because no model can be recommended without the answers:</p><ul class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground list-disc space-y-1 pl-6"><li class="pl-1"><strong class="font-semibold">Capabilities.</strong> What specific features or capabilities must the model have to meet the customer's needs?</li><li class="pl-1"><strong class="font-semibold">Speed.</strong> How quickly does the model need to respond inside the customer's application?</li><li class="pl-1"><strong class="font-semibold">Cost.</strong> What is the budget, for development and for production usage?</li></ul><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Each answer comes from the customer's own workload. Facts such as how many people work in IT, which social platforms marketing uses, or the version number of an internal ticketing tool change none of the three. They tell you nothing about what the model must do, how fast it must answer, or what it may cost.</p><aside class="my-6 rounded-lg border-l-4 border-l-primary border-y border-r border-border bg-primary/5 p-4"><p class="mb-1.5 flex items-center gap-1.5 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-primary"><svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" class="lucide lucide-target h-3.5 w-3.5 shrink-0" aria-hidden="true"><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="10"></circle><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="6"></circle><circle cx="12" cy="12" r="2"></circle></svg>On the exam</p><p class="text-sm leading-relaxed text-foreground">When asked which factors to establish before recommending a model, choose capabilities, response speed, and budget for development and production. Model trade-offs in general are covered in <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-2 hover:no-underline" href="/en/lessons/choosing-a-claude-model-lineup-trade-offs-and-starting-points/">selecting Claude models on trade-offs</a>. The skill here is reading the brief and seeing which path it already implies.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Both paths also use a second dial, <strong class="font-semibold">effort</strong>. Effort is a per-request setting that controls how much reasoning the model does before it answers. A higher level buys more intelligence and costs latency and tokens; a lower level answers faster and cheaper but thinks less. That is why "the deepest reasoning setting" suits a hard problem and wastes money on simple triage.</p><figure class="my-6"><figcaption class="mb-1.5 text-xs text-muted-foreground">Two starting paths and the discovery findings that select each</figcaption><div class="overflow-x-auto rounded-lg border border-border"><table class="min-w-full border-collapse text-sm"><thead><tr class="bg-muted/60"><th scope="col" class="px-3 py-2 text-left align-bottom font-semibold text-foreground">Path</th><th scope="col" class="px-3 py-2 text-left align-bottom font-semibold text-foreground">Discovery shows</th><th scope="col" class="px-3 py-2 text-left align-bottom font-semibold text-foreground">How it proceeds</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr class="border-t border-border align-top"><td class="px-3 py-2 text-foreground">Fast and cost-effective first, e.g. Claude Haiku 4.5</td><td class="px-3 py-2 text-foreground">Tight latency requirements, cost-sensitive budget, high-volume straightforward tasks, early prototyping</td><td class="px-3 py-2 text-foreground">Begin with it, test the use case thoroughly, check it against requirements, upgrade only for specific capability gaps</td></tr><tr class="border-t border-border align-top"><td class="px-3 py-2 text-foreground">Capability first, e.g. Claude Opus 4.8</td><td class="px-3 py-2 text-foreground">Complex reasoning, scientific work, nuanced understanding, accuracy outweighs cost, advanced coding and high-autonomy agentic work</td><td class="px-3 py-2 text-foreground">Implement with the strongest starting point, optimise prompts, evaluate, then gain efficiency by lowering effort or downgrading over time</td></tr></tbody></table></div></figure><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">Claude Haiku 4.5 suits the first path because it was built for interactive applications and high-volume processing. A support desk triaging 50,000 short tickets a day under a sub-second target is that workload. Starting there and testing on real ticket samples answers every finding at once. Starting with maximum reasoning on every request, or picking a model because of its context window, answers questions the customer never raised. Context window size is not one of the three criteria, and short triage prompts do not need a larger one.</p><p class="mt-4 leading-relaxed text-foreground">The second path fits the opposite brief. Take a research team that needs multi-step literature synthesis, says accuracy outweighs cost, and wants a long-running agent that plans its own steps.
